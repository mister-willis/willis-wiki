    Waterline Model Boat

    Rule 10 of the competition says you will draw a waterline around your boat before race day. Nobody said the line has to be right, but a line that is right is worth something: it tells you, before you are sitting in the pool, whether your boat will carry two people with room to spare or ship water over the side on the first stroke. In this lab you'll build the calculator that predicts the line, then design and print a small hull, draw its line, and float it to see how close you came. The spreadsheet predicts; the water votes.

    Overview

    You'll make a Google Sheets calculator that turns a boat's weight and footprint into a waterline height, using the three steps on the Calculate Yeer Waterline page. Then you'll design a small flat-bottomed hull in Fusion from simple shapes, 3D print it, load it with at least 150 g of washers, draw the waterline your calculator predicts, and float it. The calculator is yours to keep: you'll use it again when you size the real boat.

    This is a test hull, not your boat

    The hull you print here is a small, boring box built to test the calculation. It is not a model of your race boat. You'll design the real boat separately, later, with everything you learn here.

    Before you start

    Sign in to your school Google account and to Autodesk Fusion, and open your notebook. Have the Calculate Yeer Waterline page open in another tab; the spreadsheet follows it step for step.

    Materials

    • Chromebook with Google Sheets
    • Fusion, and access to the 3D print queue
    • Digital scale (reads in grams)
    • At least 150 g of washers
    • Calipers or a metric ruler
    • Felt-tip permanent marker
    • Tub of water and a towel

    Safety

    The printer's nozzle and bed are hot enough to burn; let a print cool before you remove it. Keep the water tub on the floor or the side table, away from Chromebooks and the printers, and dry your hands before you touch a keyboard.

    You've got it when…

    • Your spreadsheet turns any set of weights and dimensions into a waterline height and a freeboard, and recalculates by itself when you change a number.
    • Your printed hull has vertical sides, a flat bottom, and a footprint no larger than 150 mm × 200 mm.
    • Your hull has a marker line at the height your spreadsheet predicted for its real weight plus the washers.
    • You floated it, photographed it, and recorded how far the water landed from your line.

    Collaboration & AI

    Work: On your own. Compare numbers with a neighbor all you like, but build your own spreadsheet and your own hull.

    AI — AIAS Level 1, No AI: The point of this lab is to do the calculation yourself once, so that the spreadsheet is a tool you understand rather than a box that spits out a number. Set AI aside. What the levels mean.

    Build the Calculator

    A spreadsheet is a grid of cells, each named by its column letter and row number (B4 is column B, row 4). A cell can hold a label, a number, or a formula that calculates from other cells. Formulas are the whole point: change a weight, and every number downstream updates on its own.

    The sheet you'll build follows the three steps on the Calculate Yeer Waterline page, plus one row at the end for freeboard. When you're done it will look like this.

    A B C D

    2. Click the title Untitled spreadsheet in the top-left corner and rename it Waterline Calculator.

    3. Click cell A1, type Waterline Calculator, and press Enter. Pressing Enter finishes the cell and moves you down one row.

    4. Click cell A3 and type the labels for column A, pressing Enter after each one so you move down a row each time. Leave rows 9, 13, and 18 empty; they're just spacing.

    Add the Formulas

    A formula starts with =. Everything after the equals sign is math that Sheets does for you, and a cell name inside it (B4) means "whatever number is in that cell right now."

    1. Click B8 and type the total-weight formula, then press Enter.

      B8
      =SUM(B4:B7)

      SUM adds up a range of cells, and B4:B7 means "B4 through B7." The cell shows 0 for now because the weights above it are empty. That's correct.

    2. Click B12 and type Step 1 from the wiki page: displacement volume is total weight divided by water density.

      B12
      =B8/B11

    3. Click B17 and type Step 2: waterline area is length times width.

      B17
      =B15*B16

      The cell shows 0 because length and width are empty.

    4. Click B20 and type Step 3: waterline height is displacement volume divided by waterline area.

      B20
      =B12/B17

      This one shows #DIV/0!, an error, because the area it divides by is still 0. It will fix itself as soon as the area has a number.

    5. Click B22 and type the freeboard formula: side height minus waterline height.

      B22
      =B21-B20

    Why freeboard?

    Freeboard is the height of dry hull above the waterline. The wiki page stops at waterline height; this row goes one step further because freeboard is the number that answers "will I get wet?" A boat with 2 cm of freeboard is a submarine with ambition.

    A formula that shows itself

    If a cell shows the text =B8/B11 instead of a number, Sheets is treating it as a label. Usually a space or apostrophe snuck in before the =. Click the cell, delete everything, and type the formula again starting with =.

    Test It

    The best way to trust a formula is to feed it numbers you already know the answer to. The metric example on the wiki page is 145,000 g on a 180 cm × 90 cm bottom, which works out to a waterline of 8.95 cm.

    1. Type 145000 in B4, then 180 in B15 and 90 in B16. B20 should show 8.950617....

    2. Type 30 in B21. Freeboard in B22 should show 21.04938....

    3. Change B4 to 100000. Watch B8, B12, B20, and B22 all change by themselves. That is the whole reason you built a spreadsheet instead of using a calculator.

    4. Delete the test numbers from B4, B15, B16, and B21 so the sheet is ready for real data. Leave B11 as 1.

    Checkpoint

    If the sheet gave you 8.95 cm for the test numbers, your formulas match the wiki page and you can trust what it tells you from here on.

    Make It Readable

    A spreadsheet should be formatted only to make it easier to read, and every color should mean something. Here, yellow means "you type here" and green means "the sheet calculates this; leave it alone."

    1. Click A1, then click the Bold button in the toolbar (or press Ctrl+B). Do the same for the four section labels in A3, A10, A14, and A19.

    2. Click B4 and drag down to B7 to select the four input cells. Click the Fill color button (the paint bucket) in the toolbar and pick a light yellow. Do the same for B15, B16, and B21.

    3. Select the five calculated cells one at a time, B8, B12, B17, B20, and B22, and fill each with a light green.

    4. Click B20, then click Format ▸ Number ▸ Number. It now shows two decimal places, which is as much precision as a felt marker can use. Do the same for B22.

    5. Drag the line between the column A and column B headers to the right until every label in column A fits on one line.

    Your screen should look like this.

    Units for the real boat

    This sheet is metric: grams and centimeters, so water density is a convenient 1. When you size the real boat, people will know their weight in pounds and you'll likely measure the cardboard in inches. Convert before you type: 1 lb = 453.6 g and 1 in = 2.54 cm. Or add a conversion row of your own; a formula like =B4*453.6 is exactly the kind of thing a spreadsheet is for.

    Planing Hulls Are Made of Simple Shapes

    The sheet so far assumes a rectangular bottom. Real boats rarely cooperate. A planing hull is a flat-bottomed hull with vertical sides, the kind cardboard builds well, and its footprint can be any shape you can cut from a sheet. The slide you saw in class shows five of them.

    The trick is that every one of those footprints is a few simple shapes glued edge to edge. Cut the pointed bow off a hull and what's left is a rectangle; the piece you cut off is a triangle. The waterline area is the sum of the pieces. Here are the pieces you'll need.

    Shape Area formula In Sheets
    Rectangle length × width =L*W
    Triangle ½ × base × height =0.5*B*H
    Circle π × radius² =PI()*R^2
    Half circle ½ × π × radius² =0.5*PI()*R^2
    Trapezoid ½ × (top + bottom) × height =0.5*(T+B)*H

    Replace L, W, B, H, R, and T with the cells that hold those numbers. The "height" of a triangle or trapezoid is measured straight across, at a right angle to its base, not along the slanted edge.

    And here is how the five hulls from the slide come apart.

    Hull Pieces Waterline area
    Rectangle 1 rectangle L × W
    Pointed bow rectangle + triangle (L × W) + (½ × W × bow length)
    Hexagon rectangle + 2 triangles (L × W) + 2 × (½ × W × point length)
    Stadium rectangle + 2 half circles (L × W) + π × (W ÷ 2)²
    Teardrop triangle + half circle (½ × W × nose length) + ½ × π × (W ÷ 2)²

    Worked example: the pointed bow from the slide

    The slide's pointed bow is a 40 cm × 60 cm rectangle with a 24 cm long point on the front. Rectangle: 40 × 60 = 2,400 cm². Triangle: ½ × 40 × 24 = 480 cm². Waterline area: 2,880 cm². That is 20 percent more area than the rectangle alone, which means a boat of the same weight sits 17 percent higher in the water, free of charge.

    Teach your sheet a new shape

    When your hull isn't a rectangle, add rows under Waterline Area for the extra dimensions (a bow length, a radius) and change the formula in B17 to add up the pieces. The rows below B17 don't care how the area was calculated; they just use it.

    Design and Print a Test Hull

    Now build something to test the calculator on. Keep it small, plain, and honest: a flat bottom, vertical sides, and a footprint made of the shapes above. The constraints are tight on purpose so that it prints in a reasonable time and the math stays simple.

    Constraint Limit
    Footprint fits inside 150 mm × 200 mm
    Hull height 50 mm maximum
    Floor and walls 4 mm thick
    Sides vertical, no flare, no taper
    Shape two or more simple shapes from the table above (a plain rectangle is allowed but earns no style points)
    Pieces one solid body, printed in one piece, flat bottom on the bed

    1. In Fusion, create a new design and save it as Waterline Hull.

    2. Start a sketch on the bottom plane (XY). Draw your footprint from simple shapes: a 2-Point Rectangle for the body, then a Line for each side of a pointed bow, or a Center Point Arc for a rounded end, with the shapes sharing an edge.

    3. Use Sketch Dimension (D) to set every length and radius, and check that the overall footprint fits inside 150 mm × 200 mm. Write the dimensions down; you'll need them for the spreadsheet.

    4. Finish Sketch, then Extrude (E) every profile at once to your hull height, 50 mm or less. Picking all the profiles together makes a single body.

    Your model should look like this.

    1. Click Modify ▸ Shell. Click the top face of the body to remove it, set Inside Thickness to 4 mm, and click OK. The solid block becomes an open box with a 4 mm floor and 4 mm walls, which is the hull.

    Your model should look like this.

    1. Use Inspect ▸ Measure to confirm the outside length and width and the wall thickness. These outside dimensions are the ones the water sees, so they're the ones that go in the spreadsheet.

    2. Export the body as a mesh (right-click the body in the Browser ▸ Save As Mesh, format 3MF) and submit it to the print queue the way you did for your last project. Print it flat side down with no supports; a box with vertical walls needs none.

    Print time is real time

    A 150 mm × 200 mm hull with 4 mm walls is a multi-hour print. Queue yours before the end of today's period, or it won't be dry land tomorrow. A smaller footprint prints faster and tests the math just as well.

    Why vertical sides?

    The three-step calculation assumes the waterline area is the same at every depth, which is only true when the sides go straight up. Flared sides would make the real waterline land higher than the prediction; tapered sides, lower. Vertical sides make this a fair test of the spreadsheet. The real boat can do what it likes, as long as you account for it.

    Weigh, Calculate, Mark

    Your hull is printed. Before it touches water, commit to a prediction.

    1. Start a notebook entry titled Waterline Model Boat and copy the table below into it. You'll fill it in as you go.

      Measurement Value
      Hull mass (g)
      Washer mass (g)
      Total mass (g)
      Outside length (cm)
      Outside width (cm)
      Other dimensions (bow length, radius, …)
      Waterline area (cm²)
      Predicted waterline height (cm)
      Side height (cm)
      Predicted freeboard (cm)
      Measured miss: water above (+) or below (−) the line (mm)

    2. Weigh the empty hull on the scale and record its mass in the table.

    3. Weigh out at least 150 g of washers. Record the actual mass; "at least 150" means your number is probably 150-something, and the something matters.

    4. In your spreadsheet, type the hull mass in B7 (Boat) and the washer mass in B6 (Cargo). Leave the occupant rows empty; the washers are not going to row.

    5. Measure the hull's outside dimensions with calipers in millimeters, divide by 10 to get centimeters, and enter them in B15, B16, and any extra rows you added for your shape. If your B17 formula is still =B15*B16 and your hull isn't a rectangle, fix the formula now using the area tables above.

    6. Enter the hull's side height in B21.

    7. Read the predicted waterline height from B20 and the freeboard from B22, and write both in your notebook table. Take a screenshot of the spreadsheet showing the numbers and add it to the same entry.

    Checkpoint

    A reasonable hull and 150 g of washers should predict a waterline somewhere around 1 to 2 cm. If your sheet says 15 cm, or 0.1 cm, a unit slipped somewhere. Check millimeters against centimeters before you go any further.

    1. Convert the predicted height to millimeters (multiply by 10) and mark it on the outside of the hull, measuring up from the bottom. Make a dot at each corner and at least one in the middle of each long side; for a curved end, make several dots around the curve.

      A height gauge from junk

      Set the hull on a flat table. Tape a marker to a block or a stack of washers so its tip sits exactly at the predicted height above the table, then slide the block around the hull while the tip touches the wall. It draws a level line all the way around in one pass, and it's more accurate than connecting dots.

    2. Connect the dots with the marker into one continuous line around the whole hull. This is your predicted waterline.

    Float Test

    1. Spread the washers evenly across the floor of the hull so it sits level. A pile in one corner tips the boat and puts one side's waterline higher than the other.

    2. Lower the hull gently into the tub and let it settle.

    3. Look at the line from the side, at water level. Take a photo that shows the marker line and the water surface clearly, and add it to your notebook entry.

    4. Measure how far the water surface landed above or below your line, in millimeters, and record it as the last row of your table. Above the line counts as positive; below as negative. Within 2 mm is a very good day.

    5. Below the table in the same entry, write one or two sentences on why the water missed your line in the direction it did (or why it didn't miss). Measurement error, a wall thicker than you measured, water that crept into the hull, and a lopsided washer pile are all fair suspects.

    6. Finish the entry with the wrap-up questions from class, each with a short answer:

      • A boat farther out of the water has less drag and moves faster. Looking at the spreadsheet, what can we control in the design of our boat to reduce the height of the waterline?
      • Why does a boat float while an anchor sinks?

    7. Dry the hull and put the washers back where they came from.

    Why does any of this work?

    Archimedes' principle: a floating object pushes aside a volume of water that weighs exactly as much as the object does. The hull sinks until the water it has displaced weighs as much as the hull plus its washers, and not one gram more. The spreadsheet just works out how deep a box has to sink to displace that much. The anchor question in step 43 is the same principle read the other way.

    Challenges

    Try these on your own if the water agrees with your line.

    • Lose the extra. Add washers 20 g at a time and watch the waterline climb. At what total mass does the freeboard hit zero? Predict it with the sheet first, then find out.
    • Sideways. Shift all the washers to one end. How far does the bow rise and the stern sink? The spreadsheet can't predict this; what would it need to know?
    • Two-shape swap. Change your B17 formula to pretend your hull has a stadium footprint of the same length and width. How much does the predicted waterline change? That difference is what a bow shape buys you.

    Turn It In

    • A link to your Waterline Calculator spreadsheet. Click Share in the sheet, set Anyone at Northland Prep with the link to Viewer, and Copy link. In the Google Classroom assignment, click Add ▸ Link and paste it. Do not use Add ▸ Google Drive to attach the sheet itself; turning in the file hands it over, and you need to keep it for the real boat.
    • Your notebook entry Waterline Model Boat, containing the completed measurement table, a screenshot of your spreadsheet with your hull's numbers in it, a screenshot of your Fusion sketch with its dimensions, the photo of your hull floating with the line visible, your one or two sentences on the miss, and your answers to the two wrap-up questions.

    How It's Graded

    This lab is worth up to 4 points. One score covers everything you turn in.

    Score What it looks like
    4 — Excellent The shared spreadsheet recalculates from formulas, with inputs and results visually distinguished, and its area formula matches the shape you actually printed. The hull meets every constraint. The notebook entry has the full table, both screenshots, the float photo with the line visible, a measured miss in millimeters, and a plausible one-sentence cause. The prediction was written down before the hull got wet, and the wrap-up answers use the spreadsheet (what you can change) and Archimedes (why anything floats) rather than a guess.
    3 — Above Average Everything above is present and the hull floats on or near its line, with a slip or two: a constant typed where a formula belongs, a missing unit, a blurry photo, or a wrap-up answer that is right but thin.
    2 — Average Real gaps. The spreadsheet works but the hull was never floated, or it floated but the notebook is missing the table, the photo, or the miss; or the area formula is for a rectangle on a hull that isn't one.
    1 — Below Average A spreadsheet with numbers typed in place of formulas, or a hull with no line, or a notebook entry that only has a photo.
    0 — Failing Nothing shared, nothing in the notebook, or no evidence the work was done.
