    Toy Block

    This is a 2x4 toy block. Its manufacturer's lawyers would like everyone to call it exactly that, and we are happy to oblige. Today you'll model one in Autodesk Fusion, dimensionally accurate to the real thing, using the four tools that show up in 90% of all 3D designs.

    Overview

    Nearly every model you will ever make in Fusion follows the same rhythm: draw a flat sketch, pull it into a 3D body, and refine it. In this lab you'll follow that rhythm four times over to build a toy block — base, studs, hollow underside, and rounded edges — and every dimension will match the brick in your junk drawer at home.

    The Tools You'll Meet

    This lab introduces the core toolbox. Skim the tables now; each tool is explained again at the step where you first use it.

    Modeling tools:

    Tool What it does Where to find it
    Create Sketch Starts a 2D drawing on a flat plane or face Toolbar, or the Create menu
    2-Point Rectangle Sketches a rectangle from two corners Sketch toolbar
    Center Diameter Circle Sketches a circle from its center Sketch toolbar, or C
    Line Sketches a line; doubles as a construction line in Construction mode Sketch toolbar; X toggles Construction
    Sketch Dimension Locks a size or distance with a number Sketch toolbar, or D
    Offset Sketch Copies sketch geometry a set distance from the original Sketch toolbar
    Extrude Pulls a 2D profile into a 3D body Solid toolbar, Create menu, or E
    Rectangular Pattern Repeats a feature in rows and columns Solid toolbar
    Shell Hollows out a solid body, leaving walls of a set thickness Modify ▸ Shell
    Fillet Rounds off a sharp edge Modify ▸ Fillet, or F

    Interface features:

    Feature What it's for
    Browser The design's table of contents: sketches, bodies, units, and visibility eyeballs
    Origin planes The three starting planes (XY, XZ, YZ) that align with the axes
    ViewCube and Home icon Spin the view to a standard angle; Home resets to the 3D perspective
    Parametric Timeline Records every feature you create, in order, and lets you edit any of them later
    Sketch Palette Sketch options like Construction and Look At
    Data Panel Your saved cloud files, organized into Projects and Folders

    Before you start

    Sign in to Autodesk Fusion with your Autodesk account. Your work saves to Autodesk's cloud, so you can pick it up from any computer in the lab.

    You've got it when…

    • Your block measures 31.8 x 15.8 x 9.6 millimeters, with eight studs on top made from one extrude and one pattern.
    • The underside is hollowed to a 1.49 mm wall, with three columns down the center.
    • The stud, block, and column edges are rounded with a 0.2 mm fillet.
    • The design is saved to your Fusion project as 2x4 Toy Block.

    Collaboration & AI

    Work: On your own. Ask a neighbor for a hand if you're stuck, but drive Fusion yourself.

    AI — AIAS Level 1, No AI: This lab is about learning the tools by using them, so set AI aside this time. What the levels mean.

    Start a Part Design

    Every new Fusion file begins with a choice about what you're making.

    1. Open Fusion and create a new design. Fusion asks you to choose a design intent. Choose Part Design.

      The three design intents

      A Part Design is a single manufacturable object — like today's toy block. An Assembly Design combines multiple parts into a larger object, the way individual blocks build a structure. A Hybrid Design is the classic Fusion style, where one file can hold both. We'll start with parts and work up to assemblies later. You can change your mind later by converting the Design Type in the Browser.

    2. Reset Fusion to its default settings so your screen matches this lab. Click your profile icon, click Preferences, and click the Restore Defaults button in the lower left of the window.

      Tip

      If Restore Defaults is grayed out, you're already at the defaults and there's nothing to do.

    3. While still in Preferences, set the default units. On the left side, under Default Units, select Design and set the units to millimeter. Click Apply, then close the window.

      Default units vs. this file's units

      The Preference sets the unit for every new design file. Any single file can be changed at any time: in the Browser, open the Document Settings folder and click Change Active Units. Leave everything in millimeters for this unit — all of our dimensions are metric.

    Sketch the Base

    Nearly all Fusion workflows start with a 2D sketch that you later pull into a 3D body. The block's footprint comes first.

    1. Click Create Sketch in the toolbar.

    2. Fusion asks you to pick one of the three origin planes. Select the bottom XY plane — picture it as the floor the block sits on. Fusion rotates your view to face the sketch straight on, and a Sketch tab appears in the toolbar.

      Why the straight-on view?

      Sketching in a straight-on view is an industry best practice. It removes perspective distortion, so dimensions land where you intend and your cursor doesn't snap to geometry hiding in the background.

    3. Select the 2-Point Rectangle tool.

    4. Click the origin point — the dot at the center of the screen — to place the rectangle's first corner.

      Why start at the origin?

      Starting at the origin anchors your design to a fixed point in space. An anchored sketch can't drift, and it's the first step toward a fully defined sketch — an idea we'll keep building on in the coming labs.

    5. Drag your mouse out and watch the two dimension inputs appear. Press Tab until the width input is active and type 15.8. Press Tab to lock it, type 31.8 for the length, and click in any quadrant to place the rectangle. For this block, the direction doesn't matter.

      Tip

      Typed a wrong number? Double-click any dimension to reopen it, type the new value, and press Enter.

    6. Look at your sketch lines. They changed from blue to black. In Fusion, black geometry is fully constrained — locked in place — while blue geometry can still move.

    7. Click Finish Sketch in the top right. This is your exit door back to the 3D world; Fusion needs to know you're done sketching before it hands you the 3D tools.

      Your model should look like this.

      The Browser is your table of contents

      A Sketches folder just appeared in the Browser on the left. The Browser lists everything in your design — sketches, bodies, and more — and the eyeball icons toggle visibility.

    Extrude the Base

    1. Activate Extrude — press E, or find it in the Solid toolbar or the Create menu. The sketch has only one profile (an enclosed area a 3D tool can grab), so Fusion selects it for you.

    2. Type 9.6 for the distance. The value lands in the Extrude dialog — Fusion lets you type in the canvas or the dialog, whichever is handier.

    3. The flat, straight-on view is hiding the block's new thickness. Click the Home icon next to the ViewCube to switch to a 3D perspective.

    4. Before confirming, take a minute to practice moving around. You will do this hundreds of times per lab, so it's worth making it automatic.

      To do this Do this
      Zoom Roll the scroll wheel
      Pan Hold the scroll wheel down and move the mouse
      Orbit Hold Shift and the scroll wheel together, then move
      Reset the view Click the Home icon

    5. Click OK to confirm the extrude. A Bodies folder appears in the Browser.

      Your model should look like this.

      The Parametric Timeline is time travel

      Look at the bottom of the screen. The Parametric Timeline records every feature you create, in order. Double-click any item to edit it later — change this extrude's thickness next week and the rest of the model rebuilds to match. Right-click an item for more options, like Delete.

    Save Your Work

    1. Click the Save icon in the Application bar at the top left.

      Where your files live

      Fusion stores files on Autodesk's servers, not on your computer. That's why you sign in — your designs follow your account to any machine.

    2. Name the design 2x4 Toy Block.

    3. Set up a home for this unit's work. Click the caret next to the Location field to expand the list, click New Project, and name it Fusion Labs. Save every design from this unit in this project.

    4. Add a folder inside the project: double-click the Fusion Labs project to open it, click New Folder, and name it Skill Labs.

      Warning

      This trips up nearly everyone: you must double-click the folder to open it before saving, or the file lands outside the folder. The location bar should show the folder's name before you commit.

    5. Click Save. The design tab now shows the file name and a version number — more on versioning later in the unit.

      Info

      The Data Panel, opened from the grid icon at the top left, shows all your saved files. You can also create projects and folders there and move files around.

    Add the First Stud

    The eight studs on top start as a single circle. Pattern tools will do the other seven.

    1. Click Create Sketch, then click the top face of the block. With a 3D body in place, you can sketch directly on any of its flat faces.

    2. Select the Center Diameter Circle tool or press C. Click once anywhere on the top face to place the center — don't worry about the position yet — then type 5 for the diameter and click again to place the circle. Press Esc to end the command.

    3. The circle isn't pinned down yet — click and drag it and it slides around freely. Fusion uses constraints and sketch dimensions to lock geometry in place. Activate the Sketch Dimension tool or press D.

    4. Click the circle's center, then the top edge of the rectangle, and drag to the side. A dimension appears. Click again to place it, type 3.9, and press Enter.

      Info

      No need to type "mm" — you set millimeters as the default unit in step 3, and Fusion assumes it.

    5. Repeat in the other direction: dimension from the circle's center to the neighboring edge, also 3.9.

    6. Try to drag the circle again. It shouldn't budge — the two dimensions hold it in place.

      Your model should look like this.

    7. Press E for Extrude and select the circle's profile. This sketch has more than one profile, so Fusion waits for you to pick. Type 1.7 for the distance.

    8. Before clicking OK, check the Operation field: it should say Join. Keep it. Join fuses the new cylinder and the block into one body; New Body would leave a separate, disconnected cylinder resting on top. Click OK.

      Why Join matters here

      The Shell tool you'll use shortly traces the outside contour of a body. Because the stud is joined, Shell treats it as part of that contour — which is exactly what leaves the hollow indent inside each stud, just like the real block.

    Pattern the Studs

    One stud down, seven to go. You could sketch seven more circles, but the Rectangular Pattern tool does it in one move — and if the stud ever changes, the copies follow.

    1. Activate Rectangular Pattern from the Solid toolbar.

      Pattern the feature, not the sketch

      Fusion also offers a sketch version of Rectangular Pattern. Avoid it. Patterning 3D features is far less taxing for Fusion than patterning sketch geometry, and simple sketches keep your model fast and easy to update. This is a best practice worth building early.

    2. Set the Object Type to Features, then select your stud extrude in the timeline at the bottom of the screen. The cylinder highlights blue when you have it.

    3. Click the Axes selector, then click the block's length edge first, followed by the width edge. Selecting the length first maps it to Axis 1 in the dialog, so your numbers will match the steps below.

    4. Drag the two directional arrows on the model to rough the pattern in. This visual check confirms the pattern heads across the block, not off into space, before you commit exact numbers.

    5. Set Axis 1 to a distance of 24 and a quantity of 4.

      Check the Distribution

      Make sure Distribution is set to Extent. Extent measures the total distance from the first stud to the last — not the gap between neighbors. If your preview scatters studs past the edge of the block, this setting is the usual suspect.

    6. Set Axis 2 to a quantity of 2 and an Extent distance of 8 for the second row.

      Tip

      If a pattern grows in the wrong direction, add or remove a minus sign on the distance.

    7. Click OK when the preview shows two tidy rows of four.

      Your model should look like this.

    Hollow the Bottom

    1. Click and drag the ViewCube in the top right to turn the block over. The ViewCube snaps to standard views; dragging gives you anything in between.

    2. The block is still solid. Activate Shell from the Modify menu — you're modifying an existing body, which is how Fusion organizes its menus.

    3. Select only the bottom face, then set the Inside Thickness to 1.49. Click OK.

      Your model should look like this.

      Checkpoint

      Look inside each stud: the Shell traced the joined studs as part of the outer contour and left an indent inside every one. That's the Join from step 28 paying off.

    Add the Center Columns

    A real 2x4 has three columns under the roof that grip the studs of the block below. They're built from one circle, an offset, and another pattern.

    1. Right-click the bottom inside face and choose Create Sketch — right-clicking a flat face is a second way to start a sketch. Check the Browser and turn off the visibility of your earlier sketches if they're in the way.

    2. Activate the Line tool, then turn on Construction in the Sketch Palette (or press X). A construction line draws dashed and is for reference only — it helps you line things up without adding a profile that Extrude would try to grab later.

    3. Hover over one of the recessed circles inside a stud until a circle glyph appears — that's Fusion offering to snap to the center. Click it, draw the line across the block to the center of the opposite circle, and click. Press Esc to end the Line command.

      Warning

      Snapping to existing geometry only works in a straight-on view. If your view has drifted, click Look At in the Sketch Palette to face the sketch directly.

    4. Press C for a circle, and turn Construction back off (press X). Hover along your construction line until the triangle glyph appears at its midpoint, click to place the center, and type 6.3 for the diameter.

    5. Now make the column hollow. Activate the Offset Sketch tool, select the circle, and type 1 for the offset distance. Use the Flip button until the new circle lands inside the original.

      Offset vs. Shell

      Offset is the other road to a hollow shape: it copies geometry a set distance away, giving you two profiles — a ring — to extrude directly. Shell hollows a finished body; Offset builds the hollow into the sketch. You'll use both, and today you're using each where it works best.

    6. Press E, select the ring profile between the two circles, and extrude 8.1. Click OK.

    7. Activate Rectangular Pattern again. It remembers the Features object type from last time. Select your newest extrude in the timeline, click the Axes selector, and select the block's long edge. Set the quantity to 3 and the distance to 16, then click OK.

      Your model should look like this.

    Fillet the Edges

    The shape is done; the edges are not. Real blocks are molded with slightly rounded edges, and the fillet is the tool that rounds them.

    1. Activate Fillet from the Modify menu or press F.

      Pronunciation matters

      In CAD it's FILL-it, not fill-AY. The fish gets the French pronunciation; the edge does not.

    2. Take a minute to select every edge that needs rounding: the top edge of all eight studs, the outer edges of the block, and the base edges of the three bottom columns.

      Edges, not faces

      Click carefully on the edge, not the flat face beside it. Selecting a face fillets every edge attached to that face — which usually includes edges you didn't want.

    3. Set the fillet radius to 0.2 and click OK.

    4. Save your design — same Save icon as before. Fusion stores this as a new version of the same file.

      Your model should look like this.

    Checkpoint

    Base, studs, shell, columns, fillets — one block, five features, and every tool you'll lean on for the rest of this unit. Nice work.

    Why 31.8 and not 32?

    The numbers in this lab aren't random. The real brick lives on an 8-millimeter grid — a 2x4 spans 16 by 32 mm of grid — with 0.1 mm shaved from each side so neighboring blocks don't bind. That's how you get 31.8 x 15.8, and it's a taste of a design decision you'll make in your own parts: nominal size minus clearance.

    Challenges

    Try these on your own once your block is done.

    • Time travel. Double-click the base extrude in the timeline and change 9.6 to 19.2. Watch the whole block — studs, shell, columns, and fillets — rebuild to double height. Change it back when you're done.
    • The 2x2. Model a 2x2 block from scratch, no instructions. Every dimension you need can be worked out from this lab and the 8 mm grid.
    • Reality check. Borrow the calipers, measure a real block, and compare it to your model's dimensions. Note what you find in your notebook.
