    Stepper Motors

    Overview

    Program a stepper motor to enter a combination on a paper dial. Build relative moves, track position, then move to specific dial numbers.

    Builds on

    • Analog Input and Digital Output - Your First Program

    Before You Start

    Read the motor reference and StepStick pin and current-limit guidance. Have Mr. Willis set the driver’s current limit before attaching the motor. Use full stepping throughout this lab.

    Materials

    • Arduino Uno R3, USB cable, and Arduino IDE
    • StepperOnline 17HS19-2004S1 four-wire motor and HiLetgo A4988 StepStick with heat sink
    • 12 V barrel jack screw-terminal adapter
    • 6 male-to-female jumper wires and 1 female-to-female jumper wire
    • One 10 kΩ resistor (four-band color code: brown–black–orange–gold)
    • Teacher-approved regulated 12 V DC supply (at least 1 A capacity for this unloaded trial)
    • Paper combination-lock dial sheet (PDF): print on US Letter at 100% / Actual Size; cut out one 40 mm dial per motor
    • Tape, scissors, and a tool for making the center hole
    • Multimeter and insulated adjustment tool for teacher use

    Safety

    Disconnect both power sources before rewiring

    Disconnect USB and 12 V power before changing connections. Never connect 12 V to Arduino 5V or driver VDD. Secure the motor and keep clear of its moving shaft. If it stalls or the driver becomes excessively hot, disconnect power before investigating.

    You've got it when…

    • Your wiring and teacher-checked current limit match the documented hardware.
    • You calculate the full-step pulses needed to move one number on the dial.
    • Your moveDial function accepts a distance in dial numbers and a direction.
    • Your currentPosition variable tracks the expected dial position from 0–39.
    • Your goToDial function uses moveDial to reach a target number in the requested direction.
    • Your sketch enters the combination with the required turns and pauses.

    Collaboration & AI

    Build and troubleshoot with a partner. Each student must explain the circuit and paste their own completed code into their notebook.

    AI — AIAS Level 1, No AI: Complete the code changes and explanations yourself without AI assistance. AI Assessment Scale

    Build the Circuit

    1. Disconnect all power. Wire the circuit below; use the Arduino Pinout to locate pins. Match the driver’s printed pin labels; its potentiometer faces the bottom in the diagram.

      Click to enlarge. Wire crossings without dots are not connections.

      From To
      Uno D2 STEP
      Uno D2 / STEP line Unused Uno GND in the top digital header through a 10 kΩ resistor
      Uno D3 DIR
      Uno 5V VDD
      RESET SLEEP (jumper between the two pins)
      Uno GND Driver GND beside VDD
      ENABLE Leave unconnected; the HiLetgo board pulls it LOW (outputs enabled)
      MS1, MS2, MS3 Leave unconnected; internal pull-downs select full stepping
      12 V barrel adapter positive (+) VMOT only
      12 V barrel adapter negative (−) Driver GND beside VMOT
      Motor black (A+) / green (A−) 2B / 2A
      Motor red (B+) / blue (B−) 1A / 1B

      Fit the motor connector directly over 2B, 2A, 1A, 1B, with black, green, red, blue wires from top to bottom. The driver’s GND pins connect internally; no jumper between them is needed.

      Connect the 10 kΩ resistor between the STEP pin and a GND pin on the Arduino. The resistor can go either direction.

      Why the pull-down resistor?

      During startup, reset, and uploading, STEP can float and pick up noise that causes unwanted steps. The resistor holds STEP LOW until the Arduino takes control. Only STEP needs this resistor in our tested circuit.

    2. Cut out the paper dial, make a center hole, slide it over the shaft, and tape it to the motor face. Add a small tape pointer to the shaft; keep it free to rotate.

    Make a Quarter Turn

    There are 200 pulses per motor revolution, and each STEP pulse moves the motor 1.8°. DIR selects the rotational direction (clockwise or counter clockwise). The starter sketch moves once after startup or reset.

    1. With motor power off, upload the sketch below as stepper_motor.ino and wait for it to finish running. Turn on motor power, let the shaft settle, note the pointer’s position, and press the Reset button on the Arduino once.

      stepper_motor.ino
      const byte STEP_PIN = 2;
const byte DIR_PIN = 3;
const long PULSES_PER_REV = 200L;  // Full stepping: 1.8 degrees per pulse
const unsigned int HALF_PERIOD_US = 5000;

void moveSteps(long pulses, bool direction) {
  digitalWrite(DIR_PIN, direction ? HIGH : LOW);
  delayMicroseconds(5);  // Direction settles before the first pulse
  for (long i = 0; i < pulses; i++) {
    digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, HIGH);
    delayMicroseconds(HALF_PERIOD_US);
    digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, LOW);
    delayMicroseconds(HALF_PERIOD_US);
  }
}

void setup() {
  pinMode(STEP_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(DIR_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, LOW);
  delay(2000);  // Pause before motion
  moveSteps(PULSES_PER_REV / 4, true);
}

void loop() {
  // Remain stopped. The energized driver continues to hold position.
}

    Download the starter sketch.

    1. Check for a quarter turn (10 dial numbers). Change the direction from true to false and repeat step 3. Record which value produces CW and CCW when viewed from the shaft end.

    2. With the motor stopped but powered, gently grip the shaft and try to turn it. Feel the holding torque: the energized windings resist movement. Release the shaft before continuing.

      Do not force the shaft

      Do not press Reset while gripping it or disturb the tape pointer.

    Pulse Timing and Speed

    1. Calculate pulse frequency and RPM for HALF_PERIOD_US = 5000. Change the move to a full revolution and test 2500. Compare speeds, then restore 5000. Each pulse includes a HIGH and a LOW interval:

      \[ f\approx\frac{1{,}000{,}000}{2\times\text{HALF\_PERIOD\_US}},\qquad \mathrm{RPM}\approx\frac{60f}{\text{PULSES\_PER\_REV}} \]

      Actual speed is slightly lower because of code overhead.

      Stop if steps are missed

      If the motor buzzes or misses steps, disconnect power and check wiring and load. Do not increase current or keep shortening the delay.

    Move by Dial Numbers

    1. Calculate pulses per dial number using 200 pulses per revolution and 40 positions (0–39):

      \[ \text{Pulses per dial number} = \frac{200}{40} = 5 \]

      Ten number intervals require 50 pulses. A full turn has 40 intervals, including 39 to 0.

    2. Rename moveSteps to moveDial(long numbers, bool direction). Multiply numbers by 5 to get the pulse count. Keep the direction control and pulse timing.

      Define CW and CCW constants from step 4. Update the calls in setup() and test both directions and a full turn.

      Distance, not destination

      moveDial(10, CW) moves 10 number intervals clockwise from the current position. Use a nonnegative distance; the direction argument controls which way to turn.

    Track the Current Position

    1. Add this global variable above the functions:

      int currentPosition = 0;

      Expected position

      This variable records the expected position, not a sensor measurement. Before running a positioning test, align the paper dial’s 0 with the pointer as described in the trial procedure below. Resetting the Arduino sets the variable to 0; it does not physically move the pointer to 0.

      Add this code at the end of moveDial. CW increases position; CCW decreases it. The remainder operator % wraps the result to 0–39:

      int change = numbers % 40;
if (direction == CW) {
  currentPosition = (currentPosition + change) % 40;
} else {
  currentPosition = (currentPosition - change + 40) % 40;
}

      Preserve full revolutions

      Use the original numbers value for motor pulses. Use % 40 only for position tracking. Adding 40 in the CCW expression prevents a negative remainder.

    2. Start at 0 and test these calls in order. Pause between moves and check the pointer against the expected position.

      Call Expected position afterward
      moveDial(5, CCW) 35
      moveDial(10, CW) 5
      moveDial(40, CW) 5
      moveDial(5, CCW) 0

    Go to a Specific Number

    1. Write a new function named goToDial(int target, bool direction) for targets 0–39. Calculate the distance below, then call moveDial(distance, direction).

      Direction Distance in number intervals
      CW (target - currentPosition + 40) % 40
      CCW (currentPosition - target + 40) % 40

      At the target already? Move zero intervals. For a full turn, use moveDial(40, direction). Keep the requested direction even when the other route is shorter.

      Update position once

      Only moveDial updates currentPosition and sends pulses. goToDial calls it; do not duplicate either job.

    2. Starting at 0, test these calls with pauses. Check both the pointer and currentPosition.

      Call Expected movement Final position
      goToDial(35, CCW) 5 intervals CCW 35
      goToDial(5, CW) 10 intervals CW 5
      goToDial(5, CCW) None 5
      goToDial(0, CCW) 5 intervals CCW 0

    Open the Paper Lock

    1. Plan the sequence for the combination 10–30–20:

      Stage Paper-pointer algorithm
      Establish the start Align the paper dial’s 0 with the pointer before the run; initialize currentPosition to 0.
      Clear and reach the first number Turn CCW three complete revolutions (120 number intervals), then continue CCW until the pointer reaches the first combination number. Do not reverse after the clearing turns.
      Reach the second number Reverse to CW, make one complete revolution back to the first number, then continue CW to the second number.
      Reach the third number Reverse to CCW and stop the first time the pointer reaches the third number. Do not add a full revolution.
      Finish Leave the pointer stopped at the third number. A real lock would now be ready to open.

    2. In setup(), use moveDial() for full revolutions and goToDial() for the three combination numbers. Pause one second at each number. Keep loop() empty and comment the combination, direction convention, and purpose of each move.

    3. Run each positioning test and the final sequence using this procedure. Repeat the final sequence to verify all three stops.

      Order Action
      Upload Leave motor power off. Upload and wait for the sketch to finish running.
      Power Apply motor power and let the shaft settle.
      Align Adjust the paper dial so 0 aligns with the pointer; secure it again.
      Run Press Reset once. Check the stops and directions.

      Establish zero before every trial

      Adjust the paper, not the energized shaft. If steps are missed or the pointer slips, fix the problem and realign zero before rerunning.

    Turn It In

    1. Disconnect motor power and USB. In a notebook entry titled Stepper Motors, paste your complete Arduino code that unlocks the paper lock using moveDial, currentPosition, and goToDial.
    • Complete sketch, including moveDial, currentPosition, goToDial, and the combination sequence in setup()
    • Code comments identifying the combination, direction convention, position tracking, and required full revolutions

    How It's Graded

    One score from 0–4 covers the submitted code.

    Score Evidence
    4 Excellent Complete sketch correctly converts relative moves to pulses, tracks position with wraparound, and uses goToDial through moveDial for absolute moves. The combination sequence includes the required revolutions, directions, and pauses; comments explain the position tracking and sequence.
    3 Above Average Complete, working relative and absolute movement functions, position tracking, and combination sequence, with minor omissions in comments or pauses.
    2 Average Some controlled dial motion is implemented, but significant errors remain in pulse conversion, position tracking, absolute moves, directions, or clearing revolutions.
    1 Below Average Code is largely incomplete or nonfunctional, with little evidence of the required function or combination sequence.
    0 Failing No submission or no evidence of the required work.
