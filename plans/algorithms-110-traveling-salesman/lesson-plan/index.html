    Lesson Plan — Traveling Salesman

    Planbook Fields

    Lesson:

    Direct instruction with slides on problems that defeat exact algorithms: the Traveling Salesman Problem, factorial growth (29! routes for 30 cities), and heuristics — rules of thumb that trade guaranteed-best for good-enough-fast. Lab: students play the Traveling Salesman Game at 5 cities (tie the computer), compute the path counts for 5 and 30 cities, then write two different heuristics and follow each literally through a 30-city game, with screenshots and Consider-question arguments in project notebooks. Scored 0–4 on the rubric printed in the assignment. This closes the algorithms unit.

    Standards: AP CSP AAP-4.A, AAP-2.A; AZ CS HS.IC.C.3, HS.DA.IM.1

    Learning Objectives

    Students will be able to:

    • Compute the number of possible routes for n cities and explain the factorial growth.
    • Explain why brute force stops being an option almost immediately.
    • Write a heuristic as a rule precise enough for a computer to follow.
    • Evaluate a heuristic's results as "good enough" against a stated purpose.

    Standards Alignment

    Code Standard (abbreviated) Why it's defensible here
    AAP-4.A Reasonable vs. unreasonable time; heuristics when exact solutions are impractical. The lab is this standard end to end: factorial route counts computed by hand, and two student-written heuristics standing in for the impossible exhaustive search. The unit's efficiency capstone.
    AAP-2.A Express an algorithm using sequencing without a programming language. Partial: a heuristic is a decision rule, not a full algorithm — but it must be written precisely enough to follow literally, which is the same discipline.
    HS.IC.C.3 Demonstrate ways a given algorithm applies across disciplines. The Consider questions apply the heuristic to real routing — gas money, airline tickets — and the debrief names delivery logistics and chip layout as the same problem. Flagged: the application is discussed and argued, not built.
    HS.DA.IM.1 Analyze computational models to understand real-world phenomena. Partial: the game is a model of routing cost, and students analyze their own play data (three screenshots, distances) to judge their heuristics.

    Materials & Setup

    • slides.pptx (this folder) loaded and tested.
    • The Traveling Salesman Game (Wolfram, linked from the assignment) — load it on one machine before class; it's a notebook-in-browser and takes a moment the first time.
    • Notebooks; screenshot tool (three screenshots are deliverables).
    • Calculators or Python for the factorial — 29! by hand is a punishment, not a lesson.

    Slides & Direct Instruction

    The deck (8 slides, speaker notes on each) runs: the setup — visit every city once, come home, shortest route → try it small: 5 cities, count the routes → the growth: 4! versus 29!, side by side → dark slide: 29! in seconds versus the age of the universe → heuristics defined: good enough, fast enough, guaranteed nothing → the literal-follower rule (no cheating with your eyes) → your turn: write heuristic #1 and predict its weakness → mission slide. Questions worth asking:

    • "Five cities felt easy. What exactly changed at 30?" (nothing but the count — and everything; the method that worked can't even be attempted)
    • "Is the computer's 'distance to beat' guaranteed to be the true best?" (at 30 cities it can't be exhaustive either — one of the lab's Consider questions; let them argue it)
    • "Your GPS reroutes you in two seconds. Given today's math, what must it be doing?" (heuristics — nobody is checking every route; good-enough is the product)

    Pacing — One 51-Minute Period

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–4 Settle + do-now On the board: "5 cities. From your start you pick any of 4, then 3, then 2, then 1. How many total routes?" Multiply it out — 24.
    4–16 Slides The problem, the factorial ladder, the universe comparison, heuristics. Hold the literal-follower rule until just before release — it's the rule they'll want to break.
    16–24 Game, small Play the 5-city game until tied. Screenshot. Answer question 7 in notebooks with the do-now's method.
    24–30 Write Heuristic #1 written as a followable rule, plus the practice slide's weakness prediction. Circulate: reject rules with judgment words ("best-looking," "smart choice").
    30–45 Game, large 30-city runs: follow heuristic #1 literally, screenshot; write heuristic #2 (genuinely different), follow it, screenshot. Question 10 along the way.
    45–51 Wrap Tally: whose heuristic beat the computer? (Occasionally someone does.) Cold-close: "Was yours good enough — and for what?" Close the unit: exact algorithms when you can, heuristics when you must — you now own both.

    ELEOT: The 20-Minute Window

    Item The move in this lesson
    B.2 Tie-the-computer at 5 cities is attainable; a good 30-city heuristic is genuinely hard — the ramp is built into the game sizes.
    B.4 Computing factorial growth and evaluating heuristics against a purpose are the unit's most evaluative thinking.
    B.5 The lab's numbered steps carry students through both game sizes without teacher direction; the teacher's job is enforcing literalism.
    C.2 Following a rule you can see failing — and recording the failure — is sanctioned risk; the debrief prizes honest bad routes over quietly-fudged good ones.
    D.1 The wrap tally and good-enough argument run as class exchange, anchored to each student's own distances.
    D.2 Gas money, airline tickets, GPS rerouting, delivery fleets — the lab's own Consider questions put real costs on the routes.
    D.3 Most of the period is play-measure-write, hands on the game.
    E.1 Rotating notebook check-ins against the numbered questions (7, 10, 11, 13) and the three screenshots.
    E.3 The Consider answers require verbalizing a judgment — good enough for what — not reporting a number.
    E.4 The rubric's 4 names computed-not-guessed path counts and genuinely different heuristics; quote it when a student's second heuristic is the first one wearing a hat.
    G.1 Students use the simulation to gather their own route data and evaluate strategies against it.

    Common Student Errors

    • Heuristics with judgment baked in — "go to the best next city" is not a rule a computer could follow; that's the answer, not a method. Nearest unvisited neighbor is; so is "always move clockwise."
    • Overriding the heuristic mid-game when it's about to do something dumb. The lab's warning is explicit: the moment you cheat, you're no longer measuring the heuristic.
    • Answering question 10 as "a lot" — the rubric pays for the computation (29!, and the seconds-since-the-universe comparison in the lab's hint).
    • Heuristic #2 as a reworded heuristic #1 — nearest-neighbor twice with different adjectives. Push for a different kind of rule.
    • Forgetting to return to the start city, which the game scores as an incomplete route — re-read step 5 of Starting Small.

    Assessment & Evidence

    • Deliverable: answers to questions 7, 10, 11, and 13; three game screenshots (steps 8, 12, 14); and the three Consider arguments — all in the notebook (see Turn It In).
    • Score: 0–4 holistic rubric in the assignment. A 4 computes both path counts with visible reasoning, fields two genuinely different heuristics, and argues "good enough" from the student's own routes.
    • Look-fors during check-ins: a factorial (or the multiplication chain) next to question 10; heuristics phrased as mechanical rules; screenshots whose routes visibly match the stated rule.
