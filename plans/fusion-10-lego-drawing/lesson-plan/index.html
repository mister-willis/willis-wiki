    Lesson Plan — Lego Mechanical Drawing

    The reveal is not in the student lab. The assignment stops at "put your pencil down; there's a part two." Part two is the drawing swap, and it only works if students don't know it's coming while they build and draw. Don't project this plan.

    Planbook Fields

    Lesson:

    Pencil-and-paper introduction to mechanical drawing, preliminary to the Autodesk Fusion unit. Students build a simple six-brick Lego creation, then, after slides on orthographic and isometric views, draw it on a printed drawing template (top, front, side, and isometric views with a title block). Reveal: pairs disassemble their builds, exchange drawings and bricks, and rebuild each other's designs from the drawings alone. Deliverables: the completed template and a notebook entry with photos and a reflection. Scored 0–4 on the rubric in the assignment.

    Standards: AZ CTE-ENG 3.3, 6.3

    Learning Objectives

    Students will be able to:

    • Draw the top, front, and side orthographic views of a simple object at true size on a grid, aligned so shared dimensions agree across views.
    • Draw the same object as an isometric view on a triangular grid, and explain why engineers use isometric rather than perspective.
    • Explain, from experience, what a drawing has to contain for someone else to build the part without asking questions.
    • Connect the 2D-sketch-then-3D idea to what Fusion will ask of them next week.

    Standards Alignment

    Code Standard (abbreviated) Why it's defensible here
    CTE-ENG 3.3 Display data graphically using diagrams and working drawings. The deliverable is a working drawing: three aligned orthographic views at true scale plus an isometric, with a title block. The rebuild test is the working-drawing standard applied literally.
    CTE-ENG 6.3 Apply communication skills, including listening skills, with project teams, project managers, clientele, and/or contractors. Partial: the drawing is the only communication allowed between designer and builder in part two, and the debrief is about what the drawing failed to communicate. No spoken collaboration is assessed.

    Materials & Setup

    • slides.pptx (this folder), 9 slides, loaded and tested. The deck says "next week" for Fusion; adjust the line out loud if your calendar differs.
    • Lego bin with build plates and standard bricks, enough for one plate and six bricks per student with some choice left over. Pull out the hinges, wedges, and specialty pieces before class; they invite the non-orthogonal builds that make the drawing step miserable.
    • Printed drawing templates (Lego Mechanical Drawing Template.pdf), one per student plus spares. Hand them out after the slides, not before; the sheet is the first hint that drawing is coming.
    • Pencils and erasers; rulers optional (the grid does the measuring).
    • Student phones or a class camera for the two notebook photos.
    • Notebooks.
    • Seating in pairs at tables; the swap happens within the pair.

    Slides & Direct Instruction

    The deck (9 slides, speaker notes on most) runs after the build, not before: sketch-to-solid big idea → perspective versus isometric → what "orthographic" means → one object, three views (top above front, side to the right) → orthographic versus isometric tied to the grid paper → why by hand today → next week in Fusion → mission slide. About ten minutes; the slides are the whole direct instruction. Questions worth asking:

    • Slide 3: "Which of these two could you measure with a ruler and trust?" (The isometric. Perspective converges; isometric keeps edges parallel and to scale.)
    • Slide 4: "Why does the word 'straight-on' matter to a machinist?" (Nothing is foreshortened, so a length on the page is the real length.)
    • Slide 5: "Why does the top view sit above the front view and not wherever there's room?" (They share a width; the layout is a self-check.)

    Don't mention the swap during the slides. Slide 4's "someone else can build the part from it" is the only foreshadowing, and it reads as theory until part two.

    Pacing — One 51-Minute Period

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–3 Settle + do-now Lego bin open. On the board: "Take one plate and six standard bricks. Any sizes." No mention of drawing.
    3–12 Build (steps 1–3) Students build something simple. Circulate with one refrain: "flat, studs up, square." Redirect the balanced-on-one-stud builds now; they'll thank you later. Photo of the build before moving on.
    12–22 Slides Deck, questions above. Hand out the template as the mission slide goes up. "Understand your mission for today?"
    22–38 Draw (steps 4–9) Front view first, then top above it, side to the right, then isometric. Circulate with the alignment check: "does your top view's width match your front view's?" Enforce step 9: pencils down, build stays intact.
    38–47 Part two: the swap The reveal (below). Pairs disassemble, exchange sheet and bricks, rebuild from the drawing alone. No talking between designer and builder until both are done.
    47–51 Wrap Compare rebuilds to photos. Debrief question, then the reflection in the notebook entry. Sheets handed in. Preview: "Next week Fusion draws the lines; you still draw the sketch first."

    Part two, scripted. When most pencils are down: "Take your build apart. All six bricks back on the plate, loose. Now hand your sheet and your plate to your partner." Rule: the builder may look only at the sheet; the designer may watch and may not speak. Give five to seven minutes. Then: "Designers, get your photo out. How close is it?" Most pairs get the footprint right from the top view and the heights wrong, or the depth wrong because the side view was skipped. That's the lesson, and they found it themselves.

    Debrief question for the wrap, one round of answers: "What did your partner's drawing not tell you?" Then the reflection prompt for the notebook entry: "Two or three sentences: what your drawing got across, what it didn't, and what you'd draw differently."

    This runs long if the builds are ambitious; the build timer and the "keep it square" rule are what keep it inside a period. If the swap gets cut short, do the rebuild first thing next period before the tutorials start; don't skip it.

    ELEOT: The 20-Minute Window

    Item The move in this lesson
    B.2 Six bricks and four views is attainable in a period; the alignment rules make it a real drawing rather than a doodle.
    B.3 The rubric's 4 row is "someone could build it from the sheet," and part two tests exactly that in front of the student.
    B.4 Rebuilding from a drawing is analysis in reverse: the builder has to infer depth from a side view and height from a front view.
    C.2 The build is explicitly low-stakes ("the specifics don't matter"), and the drawing is pencil on a grid with an eraser expected.
    D.1 The wrap's debrief ("what did the drawing not tell you?") is student-to-student, with the designer hearing it from their own builder.
    D.2 Slide 4's "someone else can build the part from it" is what machinists and contractors do with drawings every day; say so.
    D.3 Hands on bricks or a pencil for roughly forty of fifty-one minutes.
    D.4 The swap is structured pair collaboration with a rule (sheet only, no talking) and a checkable outcome (rebuild versus photo).
    E.2 The rebuilt model is immediate, physical feedback on the drawing; the designer sees exactly which view failed.
    E.4 Rubric in the assignment; the "could someone build it" row is stated before the drawing and enacted after.
    F.3 Four hard transitions (build, slides, draw, swap), each cued by a single instruction and a materials handoff.

    Common Student Errors

    Observed in the first run and predicted from the format; revise after the next.

    • Non-orthogonal builds. Bricks on their sides, wedged at angles, or bridging on a single stud. Observed: some students connect Lego in innovative ways, and the drawing step (which they don't know is coming) becomes much harder. The "keep it square" warning in the lab and the build-time refrain exist for this; redirect during the build, not after.
    • Drawing the isometric everywhere. Students put a three-quarter "pretty" view in the front-view panel. Ask "are you looking at it straight on?" and point at slide 4.
    • Skipping the side view. The most common omission and the one that fails the rebuild, since depth lives there. Slide 5 and step 7 both call it out.
    • Views that don't align. A top view wider than the front view. Have them lay a pencil across the sheet as a straightedge; the layout finds the error.
    • Ignoring the grid. Freehand sizes instead of counted squares. Step 4's "count squares, don't measure" and the brick dimensions on the sheet are the fix.
    • Talking during the swap. The designer coaching the builder defeats the exercise; enforce the no-talking rule and let the drawing fail if it fails.

    Assessment & Evidence

    • Deliverable: the completed paper template (handed in) and a notebook entry titled Lego Mechanical Drawing with a photo of the build, a photo of the sheet, and the reflection (see Turn It In).
    • Score: 0–4 holistic rubric in the assignment. A 4 is aligned views at true size with studs, an isometric on the triangular grid, a filled title block, both photos and the reflection in the entry, and a sheet a partner could build from.
    • Look-fors during check-ins: top view width equal to front view width; a side view present at all; stud counts that match the photo; the reflection naming a specific view that failed rather than "it went fine."
