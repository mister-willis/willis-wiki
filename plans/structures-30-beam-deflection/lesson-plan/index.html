    Lesson Plan — Calculating Beam Deflection With a Spreadsheet

    Planbook Fields

    Lesson (Day 1 — Build the model):

    Direct instruction with slides: what deflection is, the two numbers that decide a beam's stiffness (area moment of inertia from the cross-section, modulus of elasticity from the material), and the deflection formula for a center point load on a simply supported beam. Students build a Google Sheet that calculates the area moment of inertia and the deflection of a 2x4 from width, height, force, span, and a modulus looked up on MatWeb, then format it so inputs and calculated cells are distinguishable. Students finish the ruler input rows, measured-deflection formula, and percent-error formula.

    Lesson (Day 2 — Test the model):

    Students enter team measurements directly in their finished spreadsheets. Each notebook entry includes a loaded-beam photo, a screenshot of all values, the observed percent error, and possible sources of the difference.

    Standards: AZ CTE-ENG 3.1, 3.5, 3.6, 3.2, 5.4, 4.4 (partial), 3.7 (partial)

    Learning Objectives

    Students will be able to:

    • Define deflection and name the four things that change it (load, span, cross-section shape, and material).
    • Explain what the area moment of inertia and the modulus of elasticity each describe, and which one changes when a beam is turned on edge.
    • Build a spreadsheet model with formulas that reference input cells, so that changing an input recalculates the result.
    • Convert a looked-up material property from ksi to psi inside a formula.
    • Measure the deflection of a loaded beam, calculate percent error against a prediction, and name specific sources of the difference.

    Standards Alignment

    Code Standard (abbreviated) Why it's defensible here
    CTE-ENG 3.1 Use basic mathematical functions and tools (Google Sheets named). The whole lab is built in Google Sheets: cell references, exponents, parentheses for order of operations, abs(), number and percent formatting.
    CTE-ENG 3.5 Use mathematical models (algebraic relationships) to solve, analyze, and design solutions. Two algebraic models, I = wh³/12 and δ = FL³/48EI, built as live formulas and used to answer "what if" by changing inputs (the checkpoint after step 32 and the Challenges).
    CTE-ENG 3.6 Generate mathematical solutions manually and electronically and evaluate their validity. Day 2 exists to evaluate validity: the model's prediction is compared to a measurement, the error is calculated as a percentage, and students must name two reasons for the gap.
    CTE-ENG 3.2 Use data collection and analysis to display data and verify its accuracy. Students collect weight, span, and two ruler readings, enter them in their spreadsheets, and use them to check the model.
    CTE-ENG 5.4 Use software tools to solve, model, analyze, and/or design solutions (Google Sheets named). Sheets is the modeling tool; the formatting steps (right-sizing, decimals, fill color for calculated cells) are about making the model usable, not decorative.
    CTE-ENG 4.4 Analyze relevant properties of materials (mechanical). Partial. The modulus of elasticity is introduced as a mechanical property, looked up by species and moisture condition, and used; nothing is tested, and the material isn't varied in this lab.
    CTE-ENG 3.7 Use English and Metric systems of measurement. Partial. English only, but deliberately: lbf versus pounds, psi, ksi and the ×1,000 conversion, and in⁴ as a unit. Metric arrives with the bridge rig, which records in newtons and millimeters.

    Materials & Setup

    • slides.pptx (this folder), Day 1 only. Nine slides; no video, no internet needed beyond the student lookup.
    • MatWeb (matweb.com) must be reachable from student Chromebooks. Check the filter the day before; if it's blocked, the lab's collapsed tip gives the number (about 1,510 ksi) and step 27 becomes "read it off the board."
    • Notebooks (the year-long Google Doc). Have a camera available for the loaded-beam photo.
    • Day 2 hardware: one 2x4 about 8 feet long (two if the class is large; the measurement is the bottleneck), two supports of equal height and no taller than a step (stacked 2x4 offcuts or low blocks; not chairs), a tape measure, a ruler, and a bathroom scale. Set the beam up before the bell and mark the midspan yourself so the queue moves.
    • Use the measured weight of the volunteer who loads the beam.

    Slides & Direct Instruction

    The deck (9 slides, speaker notes on each, Day 1 only) runs: title → what deflection is (load on, load off) → the 2x4, nominal versus actual, flat versus on edge → shape: the area moment of inertia → material: the modulus of elasticity, as a big-number slide → the deflection formula, term by term, with its Sheets translation → "Which bends more?" prediction slide → two spreadsheet rules (formulas everywhere, color means something) → mission slide. The formulas are on the slides; the reasoning about why height is cubed and width isn't is in the notes and in your mouth. Questions worth asking:

    • On the diving board: "When you step off, is the board damaged?" (No. That spring-back is elastic deformation, which is what the modulus measures. The limit of the analogy: push far enough and it doesn't come back.)
    • On the 2x4: "Which way do floor joists go, flat or on edge? Why?" (On edge. Height is cubed in I; they'll prove the factor in the Challenge.)
    • On the modulus: "Steel is about 29,000,000 psi. Green Douglas fir is about 1,510,000. Same 2x4 shape, which sags more?" (Wood, about 19 times more. E is in the denominator.)
    • On the formula: "Double the load. What happens to deflection? Double the span?" (Doubles; eight times. Leave the second one as a prediction, since it's a Challenge.)

    Day 2 has no slides. The opener is a two-minute demo at the beam: show where the supports are, where the midspan mark is, and how the partner reads the bottom edge with the ruler upright beside the beam, then begin step 53.

    Pacing — Two 51-Minute Periods

    Day 1. Build the spreadsheet

    Minutes Activity
    0–12 Introduce bending, beam shape, material stiffness, and the prediction formula.
    12–33 Complete steps 1–32. With the starting values, C8 should show about 1.24 inches.
    33–40 Format the input and calculated cells in steps 33–43.
    40–51 Complete steps 44–52 and prepare C11 and C12 for ruler readings. Check the formulas in C9 and C10.

    Day 2. Take and interpret measurements

    Minutes Activity
    0–6 Demonstrate the two bottom-edge readings. Assign a spotter, reader, and photographer.
    6–36 Teams rotate through the scale and beam. Enter dimensions, load, span, and both readings directly into the sheet. Photograph the loaded beam.
    36–47 Add the photo and completed-sheet screenshot to each notebook. Interpret the measured error and brainstorm specific sources.
    47–51 Discuss which assumption or measurement the team would investigate next and why.

    ELEOT: The 20-Minute Window

    Item The move in this lesson
    1 The do-nows and the "Which bends more?" slide have every student committing to a prediction before the formula or the data.
    2 Day 2 measurement is done in pairs: one on the beam, one reading the ruler, then they swap.
    5 The spreadsheet itself is the organizer: labels, units, and values in columns, inputs and outputs color-coded.
    7 Sheets used to build a model that answers "what if" by changing a cell, not to type a table.
    9 Students compare a prediction to a measurement, compute the error, and have to explain the gap with specific causes.
    10 A real beam, a real scale, their own weight; deflection is the quantity the bridge rig records when their bridge is tested.
    11 The MatWeb lookup and the first formula are the stalls; the lab's warning boxes and collapsed tip let students work through them.
    15 Students report their observed error and explain what it tells them.
    18 Each day's pacing is posted; Day 2 runs a rotation through the beam while the rest of the class works on notebook evidence and optional Challenges.
    23 The "You've got it when…" list and the 1.24-inch checkpoint let students check themselves before formatting.
    24 The two reasons in the notebook entry are a written explanation of where the model and the measurement parted ways.

    Common Student Errors

    Predicted from the subject matter and a run of every step in Sheets; not yet observed in class. Revise after the first teach.

    • Formula typed without =. C4 shows the text (C2*C3^3)/12. The lab's warning box after step 20 covers it; point at the formula bar.
    • ^3 typed as 3, or C3^3 typed as C33. The result is a number, just the wrong one, so it slips past until step 32's 1.24 check. Read the formula bar aloud with them.
    • Modulus entered as 1510 instead of 1,510,000. Deflection comes out about 1,240 inches. A thousand-fold error is the ksi/psi trap; the fix is the =1000* prefix in step 28.
    • Reading MatWeb's Dry row, or a different species. A modulus of 1,830 ksi or so gives a deflection near 1.02 inches. The collapsed tip names the symptom.
    • Numerator or denominator without parentheses. =C5*C6^3/48*C7*C4 multiplies by E and I instead of dividing. The result is astronomically large, which is at least easy to notice.
    • Span measured end to end. An 8-ft board on supports 7 ft apart, with 96 entered for L, overpredicts deflection by about 50 percent. Ask students to identify the two supports used in step 55.
    • Reading the ruler at the edge of the board instead of the midspan mark, or with the ruler leaning. Both show up as an error that doesn't make sense. Have the partner hold the ruler against the side of the beam at the mark, plumb.
    • "Measurement error" as a reason. A category, not a cause. Ask students to identify a specific measurement or model assumption.

    Assessment & Evidence

    Grade the engineering notebook entry with the student rubric. Look for a photo of the loaded beam, a screenshot showing A1:C12, an accurate statement of the observed error, and specific possible sources. Credit accurate reporting regardless of the size of the error.
