    Lesson Plan — Pygame Zero: Nice Bike!

    Planbook Fields

    Lesson (Day 1):

    Direct instruction with slides on the Pygame Zero window (WIDTH/HEIGHT variables), RGB color as three 0–255 byte values, named colors as strings, and the 2D coordinate system with the origin at the upper left. Lab: students build bike.py — window, background color by RGB and by name, and practice lines and circles with the three drawing functions.

    Lesson (Day 2):

    Brief recap of coordinates and the drawing functions, then a work day on the precision challenge: reproducing a bicycle from a gridded target drawing and a set of coordinates — wheels, frame, seat tube, steering tube, handlebar, and seat, with an optional crank bonus. Scored 0–4 on the rubric printed in the assignment.

    Standards: AZ CS 6.AP.V.1, 6.DA.S.1, 6.AP.A.1

    Learning Objectives

    Students will be able to:

    • Set a window's size with the WIDTH and HEIGHT variables.
    • Build a color from red, green, and blue values, and explain why each runs 0–255.
    • Locate any point on screen with (x, y) coordinates measured from the upper-left origin.
    • Reproduce a target drawing precisely using line, circle, and filled_circle.

    Standards Alignment

    Code Standard (abbreviated) Why it's defensible here
    6.AP.V.1 Identify variables that represent different data types and perform operations on their values. The lab runs on typed variables: WIDTH/HEIGHT integers, BGCOLOR as an RGB tuple or a string — and the string-vs-tuple switch is taught explicitly (the double-quotes warning).
    6.DA.S.1 Identify multiple encoding schemes used to represent data, including binary. The "Why 0 to 255?" callout is an encoding lesson: one byte per channel, 11111111 = 255, three numbers encode any color. Binary from earlier work, cashing in.
    6.AP.A.1 Identify planning strategies to simulate algorithms that solve problems. Partial: the gridded target drawing is the plan — students translate a diagram into an ordered sequence of drawing calls, and the ones who skip the diagram redraw the frame three times.

    Materials & Setup

    • slides.pptx (this folder) loaded and tested.
    • Every student needs last lab's working setup — pgzero installed and the images folder in place. Check the stragglers list from lab 10 before the bell; a broken setup costs the whole period.
    • rgbcolorpicker.com reachable on the student network for the color-mixing segment.
    • Notebooks.

    Slides & Direct Instruction

    The deck (9 slides, speaker notes on each) runs: title → the payoff (the finished bicycle — no draw_bicycle() function exists, just you and geometry) → window variables → RGB color and the 0–255 byte → the 16.7 million colors beat (dark slide) → coordinates with the origin top-left, plus the three drawing functions → coordinate prediction practice slide → the bicycle plan (Day 2 opener: the grid, the strategy, read the hints first) → mission slide. Day 1 stops after the practice slide. Questions worth asking:

    • "What's the biggest number you can make with eight bits?" (255 — and that's the ceiling on red, green, and blue. Callback, not coincidence.)
    • "Where is (0, 0)? Which way does y grow?" (Upper left, downward — the opposite of Scratch and of math class. Say it twice; it still bites.)
    • "Which do you draw first, the white circle or the red outline on top?" (Order matters — later lines paint over earlier ones. The crank bonus depends on it.)

    Pacing — Two 51-Minute Periods

    Day 1

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–4 Settle + do-now On the board: "What's the largest value one byte can hold, and how do you know?" Binary recall in disguise.
    4–18 Slides Deck through the drawing-functions slide; mix a color live at the picker site and have the class call out channel values.
    18–24 Practice The coordinate-prediction slide, individually, then compare with a neighbor.
    24–46 Lab Build bike.py: window, background by RGB and by name, then the sample lines and circles. Fast finishers start reading the bicycle hints.
    46–51 Wrap Cold-close: "Point at where (400, 20) is on your screen." Preview: "Tomorrow, the bicycle — and it must match precisely."

    Day 2

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–4 Settle + do-now "The rear wheel is centered at (90, 180) with radius 80. What's the y-coordinate of its top edge?" (100 — warm up the arithmetic they'll use all period.)
    4–12 Slides The bicycle-plan slide: walk the grid, name the strategy (wheels → frame → tubes → handlebar → seat), and enforce "read all the hints first."
    12–44 Lab The bicycle build. Circulate; the seat and handlebar hints require deriving endpoints, which is where check-ins earn their keep.
    44–51 Wrap Side-by-side: target on the projector, volunteers' bicycles next to it. Cold-close: "Which coordinate did you have to calculate rather than copy?" Preview: "Next lab, the drawings start moving — and moving things collide."

    ELEOT: The 20-Minute Window

    Item The move in this lesson
    B.2 The challenge is scaffolded to be hard-but-doable: every anchor coordinate is given; the handlebar and seat make students derive the rest.
    B.3 The target image is the quality bar — students hold their work against it pixel by pixel and can say exactly where it falls short.
    B.4 Deriving endpoints from "the handlebar is 40 long" and "radius 80" is application-level math, not copying.
    B.5 Day 2 runs on the assignment's hints, not on teacher direction — self-directed precision work.
    C.3 Hints are ordered and numbered in the doc; stuck students are pointed to the hint number, not given the coordinate.
    D.2 RGB is the color system behind every screen they own — show the picker's values changing and name the connection.
    E.1 Rotating notebook check-ins during both lab blocks, per the course's standing system.
    E.3 Day 1's cold-close (point at a coordinate) and Day 2's (which value did you calculate?) both require verbalizing method.
    E.4 The 0–4 rubric names precision as the bar; ask a student mid-build what separates a 4 from a 3 (landing on the given coordinates, not near them).
    G.2 Two days building an original artifact in a real programming environment — the digital tool is doing the learning's heavy lifting.

    Common Student Errors

    • Swapping x and y in a coordinate pair — the line lands somewhere strange; have them read coordinates aloud as "across, then down."
    • Expecting y to grow upward — wheels drawn in the sky. The Scratch habit dies slowly.
    • Color names without quotes (skyblue instead of "skyblue") — a NameError that looks scarier than it is.
    • Radius confused with diameter — wheels twice the size they should be.
    • Diving into the bicycle without reading all the hints — the assignment warns exactly this; enforce it at the Day 2 launch, because hint 8's seat position depends on understanding hint 4's frame points.

    Assessment & Evidence

    • Deliverable: bike.py with the bicycle drawing, submitted in Google Classroom per the assignment's Turn It In.
    • Score: 0–4 holistic rubric printed in the assignment ("How It's Graded"). A 4 is a bicycle that matches the target's coordinates precisely — near enough isn't the standard, on the numbers is.
    • Look-fors during check-ins: wheels placed first and matching the spec before frame work starts; derived coordinates (seat endpoints, handlebar ends) written in the notebook or as comments rather than guessed on screen.
