    Lesson Plan — A Useful Binary Computer: The Full Adder and a 4-Bit Adder

    Planbook Fields

    Lesson (Day 1):

    Direct instruction with slides: the half adder's limit (no carry-in), the full adder as two half adders plus an OR gate, and ripple carry — chaining adders column by column the way hand addition works. Students predict the full adder's eight-row truth table, build FA in Antares from two HA components and MyOR, and verify all eight rows.

    Lesson (Day 2):

    Lab day: students wire four full adders into a 4-bit ripple-carry adder (inputs A3–A0, B3–B0; outputs S3–S0 plus Cout), grounding the first carry-in. Each student develops a three-case test plan — no carry, carry, and overflow — runs it, and submits screenshot evidence. Brief closing discussion: overflow as a real consequence of fixed-width numbers, and the unit's ladder from voltage to arithmetic. Scored 0–4 on the rubric printed in the assignment.

    Standards: AP CSP DAT-1.B, DAT-1.C, AAP-3.B; AZ CS HS.CS.HS.1, HS.AP.M.2, HS.AP.PD.1

    Learning Objectives

    Students will be able to:

    • Explain why multi-digit addition requires a carry-in, and what a full adder adds over a half adder.
    • Build a working full adder from two half adders and an OR gate, verified across all eight input combinations.
    • Chain full adders into a 4-bit ripple-carry adder.
    • Design a test plan that exercises distinct conditions, including overflow, and explain overflow as a consequence of fixed-width binary numbers.

    Standards Alignment

    Code Standard (abbreviated) Why it's defensible here
    DAT-1.B Explain the consequences of using bits to represent data (fixed-width limits). Overflow stops being vocabulary and becomes an observed event: the sum that didn't fit, on their own screen.
    DAT-1.C Calculate binary equivalents; compare binary numbers. Writing the test plan forces real conversions — students must choose 4-bit numbers that do and don't overflow, which means computing sums in advance.
    AAP-3.B Procedural abstraction manages complexity; large solutions from smaller ones. Three levels deep by now — transistors inside gates inside half adders inside FA inside the 4-bit adder — and nobody reopens a lower layer. Same cross-domain flag as labs 30–40.
    HS.CS.HS.1 Describe levels of abstraction between hardware layers. The standard's guidance names this exact endpoint — "voltage to binary signal to logic gates to adders" — and this lesson completes it.
    HS.AP.M.2 Use interrelated components/systems of parts to iteratively develop artifacts. The 4-bit adder is a system of four interacting FA modules with a defined interface (the carry chain).
    HS.AP.PD.1 Evaluate and refine artifacts; compare actual to intended outcomes. The test plan is the standard verbatim: intended sums computed by hand, actual sums observed in simulation, discrepancies debugged.

    Materials & Setup

    • slides.pptx (this folder) loaded and tested.
    • Antares with each student's working HA and MyOR — same dependency check as lab 40: a broken half adder makes Day 2 miserable. Verify stragglers early.
    • Notebooks for the eight-row truth table and the test plan.

    Slides & Direct Instruction

    The deck (8 slides, speaker notes on each) runs: "your adder stops at one digit" hook → carry-in: what hand addition does that the half adder can't → the full adder recipe (two HAs + OR) → ripple carry: the chain, column by column → the 4-bit adder layout students will wire → overflow: when 4 bits isn't enough → test plans: engineers don't hope, they check → the ladder slide: voltage to arithmetic, the whole unit on one screen. Day 1 uses through the full-adder slides; Day 2 opens on the layout slide. Questions worth asking:

    • "Add 19 + 27 by hand. Narrate the tens column — what three things did you just add?" (two digits and a carry: that's the full adder's job description)
    • "Why does the first adder's carry-in go to ground instead of nothing?" (nothing means floating — lab 30 already taught them the answer)
    • "What should a 4-bit adder answer for 9 + 9? What can it answer?"

    Pacing — Two 51-Minute Periods

    Day 1

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–4 Settle + do-now "0111 + 0011, by hand, in your notebook." Keep the carries visible — they're today's protagonist.
    4–16 Slides Through the full-adder recipe; the hand-addition narration question does most of the teaching.
    16–22 Predict Eight-row truth table in notebooks first. It's the biggest prediction of the unit — let it take the full six minutes.
    22–46 Lab Build FA from two HA components and MyOR; verify every row against the prediction.
    46–51 Wrap Cold-close: "What does the full adder accept that the half adder can't?" Preview: "Tomorrow, four of these become a machine that does real math."

    Day 2

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–4 Settle + do-now "Pick two 4-bit numbers whose sum needs 5 bits. Prove it by hand." (This becomes their overflow test case — say so afterward.)
    4–12 Slides Layout, overflow, and test-plan slides; the wiring warnings (order matters, ground the first Cin) are worth saying twice.
    12–40 Lab Wire the 4-bit adder per the assignment's staged steps; develop and run the three-case test plan; capture screenshots.
    40–51 Wrap The ladder slide. Ask the room to reconstruct it aloud, bottom up: voltage, transistor, gate, compound gate, adder. Close the unit: "You didn't draw a computer. You built one, and analyzed it to death on the way."

    ELEOT: The 20-Minute Window

    Item The move in this lesson
    B.2 The capstone build is the unit's hardest wiring, but every component in it is one the student already trusts — rigor from composition, not confusion.
    B.3 The rubric's 4 requires test cases that "genuinely exercise all three conditions" — students can articulate what quality evidence means here.
    B.4 Test-plan design is synthesis and evaluation: choosing inputs to prove a property, not just observing behavior.
    B.5 Day 2 is nearly all self-directed: staged assignment steps, self-chosen test values, self-collected evidence.
    C.3 Wiring faults hide in the carry chain; the assignment's step-by-step images plus peer debugging carry most rescues before you arrive.
    D.2 Overflow is the unit's best real-world hook: odometers rolling over, vintage game scores wrapping to zero, integer bugs in real software.
    D.3 An observer in minutes 12–40 of Day 2 sees every student wiring, testing, or documenting — engagement that needs no staging.
    E.1 Test plans in notebooks are progress monitoring students run on themselves; your rotating check-ins ride on top.
    E.3 The bottom-up ladder reconstruction at close is the whole unit verbalized by learners, not recited by the teacher.
    E.4 Grading evidence is the lab's actual subject on Day 2 — the test plan is an explanation of how the work proves itself.
    G.2 The most complex original artifact of the unit, built and validated in a digital tool.

    Common Student Errors

    • Input order scrambled — A0 wired to the bottom adder instead of the top. The adder gives confidently wrong answers; only a hand-computed expected sum catches it, which is the argument for the test plan.
    • First carry-in left floating instead of grounded — intermittent Z weirdness that students misread as a broken FA.
    • Carry chain skipping an adder, so one column never receives its carry.
    • Test cases that don't isolate conditions — e.g., an "overflow" case that also demonstrates nothing else was checked. Push for one condition per case.
    • Reading S3–S0 in the wrong order when transcribing results.

    Assessment & Evidence

    • Deliverable: eight-row full-adder truth table and the test plan in the notebook; three condition screenshots (see Turn It In).
    • Score: 0–4 holistic rubric in the assignment. A 4 requires expected sums written down in advance — evidence that checks itself.
    • Look-fors during check-ins: expected vs. actual recorded for each test; student can trace a carry from one column to the next on their own screen.
