    Lesson Plan — Professional Email (Unit Plan)

    Planbook Fields

    Lesson (introduction day):

    Direct instruction with slides on the four parts of a professional email (subject, greeting, body, closing), the four rules (short, simple sentences, scannable, proofread), and what every reader of a CC'd email can see. Students read How to Format an Email on the wiki, then write and send the first lab, Checking In, to the teacher. Scored 0–4 on the rubric printed in the assignment. Introduces the weekly routine: one email lab per week, worked independently, each adding one skill.

    Lesson (weekly, substitute the lab):

    Weekly email lab: . Students read the lab on the wiki and send the assigned email to the teacher independently, following the four-part format and the lab's specific requirements (). Scored 0–4 on the rubric printed in the assignment.

    Standards: AZ CS 6.IC.SLE.1, 6.CS.T.1, 6.AP.PD.4, 6.IC.SI.1; 7.IC.SLE.1, 8.IC.SLE.1, 7.CS.T.1, 8.CS.T.1, 7.AP.PD.4, 8.AP.PD.4, 7.IC.SI.1, 8.IC.SI.1

    Learning Objectives

    Students will be able to:

    • Write an email with a meaningful subject line, a greeting that names the recipient, a body organized into paragraphs, and a closing with their name.
    • Choose a professional tone for a request, a reply, an apology, and a pitch, and explain what changes between them.
    • Attach a file, insert an image, add a working hyperlink, and build a bulleted list using Gmail's compose tools.
    • Use To, Cc, and Reply correctly, and explain who can see whom on a CC'd email and why passwords never go in email.
    • Describe a technical problem in a form someone can act on: when it started, what they were doing, what they tried, and a picture.
    • Take a screenshot, export a Google Doc as a PDF, and copy a shareable link to a project.

    Standards Alignment

    The unit is communication practice, not programming, so the honest mappings are in Impacts of Computing and Computing Systems. Every row below names the lab that carries it. Nothing in Algorithms and Programming is claimed except the project-timeline standard, and that one is partial.

    Code Standard (abbreviated) Why it's defensible here
    6.IC.SLE.1 Describe how some digital information can be public or can be kept private and secure. Carbon Copy teaches that every recipient sees everyone CC'd and introduces BCC as the alternative; the reference page's "What never goes in an email" box and the deck's "everyone can see everyone" slide make the public/private distinction explicit.
    7.IC.SLE.1 Identify the benefits and risks associated with sharing information digitally. Same evidence as 6.IC.SLE.1, read at the 7th-grade wording: CC keeps people in the loop (benefit) and exposes addresses to everyone on the thread (risk); forwarding and passwords are named as risks.
    8.IC.SLE.1 Evaluate the benefits and risks associated with sharing information digitally. Partial: same evidence as 7.IC.SLE.1. Students apply the CC/BCC judgment in Carbon Copy and Group Email, but the unit stops short of a formal evaluation task.
    6.CS.T.1 Identify problems that can occur in computing devices and their components within a system. Asking for Tech Help: the scenario is a computer that can't reach the internet while a phone can, and the email must name when it started and what was being attempted — the standard's own example (why the internet might not work) in email form.
    7.CS.T.1 Evaluate strategies to fix problems with computing devices and their components within a system. Partial: Asking for Tech Help requires two fixes already tried, and the lab's info box names the standard first-tier strategies (restart, check the connection, try another device). Students report strategies; they don't rank them.
    8.CS.T.1 Systematically identify and develop strategies to fix problems with computing devices and their components. Partial: same evidence as 7.CS.T.1; the "what you tried" list is the checklist habit the standard's guidance describes, in miniature.
    6.AP.PD.4 Maintain a timeline with specific tasks while collaboratively developing computational artifacts. Partial: Group Email has students assign each member a task and a deadline for a (fictional) presentation. The timeline is produced; no artifact is built against it.
    7.AP.PD.4 Distribute and execute tasks while maintaining a project timeline when collaboratively developing computational artifacts. Partial: same evidence as 6.AP.PD.4 — tasks are distributed with deadlines, not executed.
    8.AP.PD.4 Distribute and execute tasks while maintaining a project timeline when collaboratively developing computational artifacts. Same partial as 7.AP.PD.4; the 8th-grade wording adds nothing this unit doesn't already ask for.
    6.IC.SI.1 Identify the advantages of creating a computational product by collaborating with others using digital technologies. Partial: Group Email and Carbon Copy use email as the collaboration tool (assigning work to a group, keeping a teacher and a parent in the loop). The advantages are modeled, not written up by students.
    7.IC.SI.1 Describe the process for creating a computational product by collaborating with others using digital technologies. Partial: Group Email is one step of that process (dividing the work by email) described in the email itself.
    8.IC.SI.1 Collaborate with contributors by using digital technologies when creating a computational product. Partial: same evidence as 7.IC.SI.1; the collaboration is real (classmates receive the email) but the product is fictional.

    Considered and left out: 6/7/8.AP.PD.2 (incorporate existing media and give attribution). Three labs link and credit a source, which is attribution in spirit, but the standard is about media incorporated into programs; mapping it here would be the cross-domain stretch worth red-penning before it's made.

    Materials & Setup

    • slides.pptx (this folder) for the introduction day. No deck for the weekly labs; the lab page is the instruction.
    • Student Gmail accounts working and signed in. Confirm before the intro day that students can send to swillis@northlandprep.org and that the account isn't restricted to internal mail for the Group Email lab (classmates are internal, so this is usually fine).
    • A Gmail label or filter for the unit (for example, filter on subject How the Year Is Going for lab 10) saves grading time; every lab names its deliverable as an email to you.
    • Carbon Copy needs parent or guardian emails. Infinite Campus has them; decide ahead of time whether students look them up or you do.
    • Technology Reflection as a PDF assumes Google Docs; Adding an Attachment assumes Google Drawings. Both are standard on the Chromebooks.
    • The reference page How to Format an Email lives at /labs/email-00-…/; every lab links to it, so it's the one page to keep current.

    Slides & Direct Instruction

    The deck (8 slides, speaker notes on each) runs: why an email is a four-second job application → the four parts, each shown on one anatomy slide → the four rules as tiles → a dark "everyone can see everyone" slide on To/Cc/Bcc and what never goes in email → a before/after fix-this-email practice slide → the weekly routine and how a lab is scored → mission slide for Checking In. The deck is for the intro day only; after that, students run the labs themselves. Questions worth asking on the way through:

    • "You get an email with no subject from an address you don't recognize. What do you do with it?" (slide 2 — delete it, which is what happens to theirs)
    • "Who can see the Cc line?" (slide 6 — everyone; then "so who should be on it?")
    • "What's wrong with this one?" on the practice slide (slide 7 — no subject, "hey," one block of text, text-speak, no name; let them find all five)

    Pacing — Introduction, One 51-Minute Period

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–4 Settle + do-now On the board: "Write the subject line you'd use to email a coach about missing practice Thursday." Cheap, and it surfaces who writes "practice" and who writes "Missing Practice Thursday - Jordan Smith."
    4–20 Slides Direct instruction with the deck. Work the fix-this-email slide live: students call out the problems before you reveal the fixed version.
    20–26 Read the reference page Students open How to Format an Email on the wiki and read it. Ask for one thing on the page the slides didn't say (the "What never goes in an email" box, usually).
    26–46 Lab Students write and send Checking In. Circulate: the first ten emails out the door tell you whether the greeting and subject landed. Rotating notebook check-ins can run as usual; this unit has no notebook deliverable, so use the time for the email itself.
    46–51 Wrap Cold-close: one student names the four parts; another says what "Re:" means (preview of lab 40). Set the routine: "Every week there's one email. Each one adds one skill. By the last one you'll be able to pitch a school event like you work here."

    Pacing — The Weekly Routine

    After the intro day, each lab is a 15–20 minute block once a week, run by the students from the lab page. A suggested shape for the block:

    Minutes Segment What happens
    0–2 Assign Name the lab, say the one new skill in a sentence ("this week you'll CC a parent"), and point at the wiki. "Understand your mission for today?"
    2–17 Write and send Students read the lab and send the email. Circulate for the lab's specific trap (see Common Student Errors); most labs have one.
    17–20 Spot check Open your inbox on the projector (with student names hidden, or just read subject lines aloud) and show two good subject lines. It's the fastest feedback in the unit and costs nothing.

    The labs are sequenced so each adds one skill: format (10) → tone (20) → paragraphs (30) → replying (40) → attachments (50, 60) → CC (70) → lists (80) → links (90, 100, 110) → multiple recipients (120) → tone under pressure (130, 140) → file conversion (150) → everything at once (160). Carbon Copy (70) reflects on "the project you just finished," so schedule it right after a project wraps (the Scratch game for P3). Winter Extravaganza (160) is the unit final and takes a full period.

    ELEOT: The 20-Minute Window

    On the intro day, the plan above does most of this on its own. During a weekly block, the window is short, so the deliberate moves matter more.

    Item The move in this lesson
    1 The fix-this-email slide has students calling out problems before the fix is shown; the do-now has everyone writing a subject line.
    5 The reference page is open in a tab while students write; the lab's "You've got it when…" box is a checklist they tick against their draft before sending.
    7 Gmail's compose tools (attach, insert photo, link, bulleted list, Cc) are the lesson, used to produce something real rather than practiced in isolation.
    9 Persuade the Principal requires evidence for a claim; Summarize a WIRED Article separates summary from reaction; Owning a Mistake asks for accountability in one sentence — judgment, not fill-in-the-blank.
    10 Every prompt is a real email students will send within a few years: asking for an extension, apologizing for a wrong file, asking for tech help, pitching an event. Say so out loud on the intro day.
    12 The weekly routine is self-run from the wiki; students read the lab, build the email, and check it against the criteria without a teacher walkthrough.
    16 Each lab carries an info box (the why) and a tip box (the how in Gmail); "ask a neighbor for the key" is explicitly legal in Sending a Screenshot.
    17 Every email in the unit is written to the teacher, and the tone standard is stated as "comfortable having it read aloud to your parents." The spot-check of good subject lines on the projector is affirming by design.
    18 The weekly block is three segments on a clock; the lab page is the instruction, so the transition is one sentence.
    21 The 0–4 rubric is printed on every lab; the projector spot-check gives feedback against it within minutes of sending.
    23 The "You've got it when…" checklist on each lab is the self-assessment, and Winter Extravaganza tells students to go down the list before sending.

    Common Student Errors

    • No subject, or a subject that's a greeting ("hi mr willis"). The fix is the do-now on the intro day and the projector spot-check every week.
    • One block of text. Students press Enter once and get a new line, not a new paragraph. Your Weekend in Two Paragraphs exists to catch this; show the double-Enter on the projector.
    • "Attached" with nothing attached. Happens in 50, 60, 140, 150, and 160. The labs tell them to attach before writing the body; say it again.
    • Retyping the subject on a reply or adding words to it (lab 40). The rubric checks for the exact Re: subject.
    • Teacher in To instead of Cc on Group Email, or a parent in To on Carbon Copy. Have students screenshot the address fields if they're unsure before sending.
    • Hand-typed dashes instead of a formatted list (80, 160). Point at the underlined A at the bottom of the compose window.
    • Raw URLs where a linked phrase was asked for (90 onward), and links that don't work because they were copied from the wrong tab. "Click your own link before you send."
    • Blame-the-computer apologies in Owning a Mistake ("the file got attached"). The lab's info box models the one-sentence version.
    • Vague tech-help emails in 140 ("it isn't working"). Insist on the timeline, the goal, and two fixes.

    Assessment & Evidence

    • Deliverable: one email per lab, sent to swillis@northlandprep.org (CC'd on Group Email), with whatever attachment, link, or CC the lab names. See each lab's Turn It In; there is no notebook deliverable in this unit.
    • Score: 0–4 holistic rubric printed in each lab ("How It's Graded"). A 4 requires all four parts, every lab-specific item, and clean mechanics; the rubrics are written so the lab's one new skill (the CC, the list, the link, the attachment) is what separates a 4 from a 3.
    • Look-fors during check-ins: the subject line before anything else; paragraph breaks; the address fields on the CC labs; the attachment icon before the send button is pressed. If a student's notebook comes up in the rotation during a weekly block, the email unit has nothing to show there by design; look at their sent mail instead.
